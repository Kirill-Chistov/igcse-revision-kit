## 1. Globalisation (1.5.1)

::: definition
**Globalisation** — the process by which the world's economies become increasingly connected, through growing trade in goods and services and the movement of money, people and ideas between countries.
:::

It has grown because of cheaper transport, the internet and trade blocs removing barriers to trade.

:::: grid
::: advantages | Opportunities for businesses
- Bigger, worldwide market → more sales; even small businesses can sell abroad online.
- Can buy materials or produce where it is cheaper → lower costs.
- Larger output → economies of scale (→ 5.1).
:::
::: disadvantages | Threats to businesses
- More competition from foreign businesses → lower prices or lost market share.
- Exchange rate changes make prices and costs uncertain (§4).
- Differences in language, culture and laws → extra costs and risk.
:::
::::

## 2. Multinationals (1.5.2)

A multinational has production or service operations in more than one country (→ 1.2.3). The country where it sets up is the **host country**.

:::: grid
::: advantages | Benefits of becoming a multinational
- Access to a bigger market → more sales and revenue.
- Lower costs: cheaper labour, materials or land abroad.
- Producing inside a country or trade bloc avoids tariffs and cuts transport costs.
- Risk is spread: weak sales in one country can be offset by others.
:::
::: disadvantages | Drawbacks for the business
- Harder to communicate with and control operations in distant countries.
- Different laws, cultures and languages → costly mistakes.
- Exchange rate changes affect costs and profits.
:::
::::

:::: grid
::: advantages | Benefits to the host country
- Jobs → lower unemployment and higher incomes.
- Tax revenue for the government.
- Investment in infrastructure and technology; training raises workers' skills.
- More choice for consumers; exports from the new factories.
:::
::: disadvantages | Drawbacks to the host country
- Profits are sent back to the multinational's home country.
- Local businesses cannot compete and may close.
- Senior jobs often go to foreign managers; local jobs may be low-paid.
- Environmental damage and use of natural resources; it may leave suddenly.
:::
::::

## 3. Exchange rate calculation (1.5.3)

::: definition
**Exchange rate** — the price of one currency in terms of another, e.g. £1 = US$1.25.
:::

![Converting between two currencies](assets/exchange_conversion.svg)

*Figure 1. Converting with an exchange rate.*

::: card | Worked example — £1 = US$1.25
A UK business sells a jacket for £80. Price in the USA = 80 × 1.25 = **US$100**.

It buys US materials costing US$500. Cost in pounds = 500 ÷ 1.25 = **£400**.
:::

## 4. Impact of exchange rate changes (1.5.4)

::: definition
**Appreciation** — a rise in the value of a currency against another currency (£1 buys more dollars).

**Depreciation** — a fall in the value of a currency against another currency (£1 buys fewer dollars).

**International competitiveness** — the ability of a business to sell its products abroad at prices that compare well with foreign rivals.
:::

**Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries; **imports** are those bought from other countries.

| | Appreciation, e.g. £1 = $1.25 → $1.50 | Depreciation, e.g. £1 = $1.25 → $1.00 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Price in the USA of a £100 export** | $125 → $150: dearer | $125 → $100: cheaper |
| **Exporters** | foreign demand falls → fewer sales; or they cut the £ price and accept less profit per unit | foreign demand rises → more sales; or they keep the $ price and earn more £ per unit |
| **Importers** | imported goods and materials are cheaper → lower costs, higher profit or lower prices | imports are dearer → costs rise → higher prices or less profit |
| **International competitiveness** | falls | rises |

A business that imports its materials **and** exports its products is affected both ways.

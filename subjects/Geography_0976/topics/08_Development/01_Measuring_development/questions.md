## Questions

1. **[2 marks]** *Define* gross national income (GNI) per capita.
2. **[1 mark]** *State* which indicator of development is **lower** in more developed countries.
3. **[3 marks]** *Identify* the three dimensions combined in the Human Development Index.
4. **[2 marks]** *Explain* why economic indicators are given per capita.
5. **[3 marks]** Using Figure 2 in the notes, *describe* the relationship between GNI per capita and
   life expectancy.
6. **[2 marks]** *Suggest* why South Africa has a lower life expectancy than some LICs.
7. **[4 marks]** Country A: GNI per capita $1,050, infant mortality 52. Country B: GNI per capita
   $48,000, infant mortality 3. *Compare* the two countries' levels of development using the data.
8. **[6 marks]** *Evaluate* the usefulness of GNI per capita as an indicator of development.
9. **[4 marks]** *Explain* the difference between quality of life and standard of living.
10. **[4 marks]** *Explain* two factors, other than income, that affect quality of life.

## Answers and marking guidance (not official)

1. Total income earned by a country's people and businesses, including from abroad, in a year (1)
   divided by the population (1).
2. Infant mortality rate (accept people per doctor).
3. Life expectancy / health (1); education — years of schooling (1); income — GNI per capita (1).
4. Countries have very different populations (1); dividing by population shows wealth per person
   so countries can be compared fairly (1).
5. Positive correlation (1); steep rise at low incomes (below ~$15,000) (1); levels off at high
   incomes, around 80–84 years (1); credit a data quote or an anomaly (e.g. USA).
6. Any two linked: high HIV/AIDS rates; high inequality — wealth not reaching the poor; unequal
   access to health care.
7. B's GNI per capita is about 46 times A's (1, with data); A's infant mortality is 17 times
   higher (1); both indicators show B more developed (1); comment that A is an LIC (≤ $1,175) and
   B an HIC, or that two indicators give a fuller picture (1).
8. Up to 3 for usefulness (easy to collect/compare; shows spending power for services; basis of
   World Bank groups) and up to 3 for limitations (average hides inequality; misses informal and
   subsistence economy; exchange rates ignore cost of living; says nothing about health, education,
   freedom). Top marks need a judgement: useful as a starting point but should be used with social
   and composite indicators.
9. Standard of living = material wealth: income, goods and services a person can afford (1–2);
   quality of life = overall well-being, including non-material factors such as health, safety,
   freedom, environment (1–2); it is partly subjective (1).
10. Each factor named (1) and explained (1), e.g. pollution → poor health; crime/conflict → fear,
    lack of safety; access to health care → longer, healthier life; political freedom; leisure time.

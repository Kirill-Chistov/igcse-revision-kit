## 1. The three wires (2.2)

Mains electricity is supplied at about **230 V**. A mains cable has three wires:

| Wire | Colour | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **live** | brown | carries the high alternating voltage to the appliance — the dangerous wire |
| **neutral** | blue | completes the circuit; stays close to 0 V |
| **earth** | green and yellow | joined to any metal case; carries a current **only when there is a fault** |

The **fuse** (or circuit breaker) and the **switch** are always in the **live** wire, so that breaking
the circuit disconnects the appliance from the high voltage. In the neutral wire they would leave the
appliance live even when it is off.

## 2. Protecting the device and the user (2.2)

| Feature | How it works | What it protects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Insulation** | Plastic covers every wire. Plastic is an insulator, so no current can pass to a person who touches the cable. | the **user** (no shock) |
| **Double insulation** | Insulated wires **and** an insulating (plastic) casing, with no touchable metal part that could become live. Needs **no earth wire** (two-core cable). Marked with a square inside a square. | the **user** |
| **Earthing** | The earth wire joins the metal case to earth, so a fault current flows to earth and blows the fuse (Figure 1). | the **user** — the case cannot stay live |
| **Fuse** | A thin wire in the live wire. If the current is bigger than its rating, the fuse wire **melts** and breaks the circuit. It must be replaced. | the **device and its cable** — stops overheating and fire; with earthing, the user too |
| **Circuit breaker** | An automatic switch in the live wire that **opens (trips)** when the current is bigger than a set value. It acts faster than a fuse and is **reset**, not replaced. | as a fuse |

![A mains supply connected to an appliance with a metal case: the live wire (brown) runs through a fuse to the heating element, the neutral wire (blue) returns from it, and the earth wire (green and yellow) is joined to the case and to earth. A fault makes the live wire touch the case. Steps: 1 the live wire touches the metal case; 2 the earth wire has a low resistance, so a large current flows through it to earth; 3 the large current melts the fuse or trips the circuit breaker; 4 the live wire is cut off and the case is safe to touch. Without an earth wire the case stays at 230 V, and a person who touches it becomes the path to earth and gets an electric shock.](assets/fig_earthing_fuse.svg)

*Figure 1. Earthing and a fuse working together when a fault makes the case live.*

## 3. Why a current heats a resistor (2.3)

![Inside a metal resistor: free electrons drift from negative to positive, opposite to the conventional current, and collide with the vibrating positive metal ions. In each collision energy passes to an ion, which vibrates more, so the temperature of the resistor rises.](assets/fig_heating_effect.svg)

*Figure 2. The heating effect of a current.*

Energy is transferred **electrically** from the supply to the **thermal** energy store of the
resistor, which then heats its surroundings. The bigger the current, the greater the heating.

**Uses in the home.** Every appliance with a **heating element** (a resistance wire): kettle,
toaster, electric heater, hairdryer, iron, oven, immersion heater. The filament of a filament lamp
gets hot enough to glow.

## 4. Power and choosing a fuse (2.4)

::: note | Equation — recall
**power = current × voltage**  *P* = *I* × *V*

*P* in watts (W) · *I* in amperes (A) · *V* in volts (V). Power is the energy transferred each second.
:::

**Choosing a fuse**

1. Work out the normal current: *I* = *P* ÷ *V*.
2. Choose the fuse with the **lowest rating that is above** that current (common ratings 3 A, 5 A, 13 A).

::: note | Worked example
A 2.8 kW kettle on 230 V: *I* = 2800 ÷ 230 = **12.2 A** → **13 A fuse**.

A 60 W lamp on 230 V: *I* = 60 ÷ 230 = **0.26 A** → **3 A fuse**.
:::

A rating below the normal current and the fuse melts in normal use. A rating far above it and the
fuse may not melt when a fault makes the current too big, so the cable can overheat.

## 5. Energy transferred (2.5)

::: note | Equation — given in the exam
**energy transferred = current × voltage × time**  *E* = *I* × *V* × *t*

*E* in joules (J) · *I* in A · *V* in V · *t* in **seconds**. Since *P* = *I* × *V*, this is also *E* = *P* × *t*.
:::

::: note | Worked example
A heater takes 8.0 A from the 230 V mains for 15 minutes:
*t* = 15 × 60 = 900 s, so *E* = 8.0 × 230 × 900 = 1 656 000 J ≈ **1.7 MJ**.
:::

## 6. Alternating and direct current (2.6)

![Two voltage–time graphs. Direct current from a cell or battery: a horizontal line at a steady positive voltage, so the current is in one direction only. Alternating current from the mains: a wave that goes positive then negative, so the current reverses direction every half-cycle.](assets/fig_ac_dc.svg)

*Figure 3. Voltage against time for d.c. and a.c.*

::: definition
**Direct current (d.c.)** — current that flows in **one direction only**; supplied by a **cell or battery**.

**Alternating current (a.c.)** — current that **repeatedly reverses direction**; **mains electricity** is a.c.
:::

UK mains is a.c. at **50 Hz**: 50 complete cycles (forwards, then back) every second.

::: traps
| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| The earth wire carries a current all the time. | Only when there is a **fault**. |
| A fuse protects you from electric shocks. | It melts only at large currents, to stop **overheating**; it protects you only together with the **earth wire**. |
:::

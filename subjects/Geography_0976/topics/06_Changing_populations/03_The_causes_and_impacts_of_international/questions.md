# The causes and impacts of international migration — Questions

Specification points 6.3.1–6.3.5. Cambridge command words. Marks in brackets.
These are practice questions written for this repository, not official Cambridge papers;
the guidance below shows the kind of point that earns credit.

## 1. Recall and definition

1. Define **economic migrant**. (1)
2. State the difference between an **asylum seeker** and a **refugee**. (2)
3. Give **two** pull factors that attract migrants to a destination country. (2)
4. What is meant by **remittances**? (1)

## 2. Understanding and explanation

5. **Explain** why most refugees live in countries neighbouring their own. (3)
6. **Explain** the push factors that cause people to leave low-income countries. (4)
7. **Explain** the positive impacts of emigration on the country of origin. (4)
8. **Explain** the negative impacts of migration on the migrants themselves. (4)
9. **Explain** how immigration can benefit a country with an ageing population. (3)
10. **Describe** how a points-based immigration system works. (3)

## 3. Evaluation and judgement

11. **Evaluate** the use of border controls to manage international migration. (6)
12. **Evaluate** the strategies and techniques used to manage international migration. (8)

## 4. Detailed specific example

13. For a named international migration you have studied, describe its push and pull factors, its impacts on the migrants, the country of origin and the destination country, and how it is managed, including how sustainable that management is. (8)

## Answers and marking guidance

1. A person who moves voluntarily to another country to find work or earn higher wages (1).
2. An asylum seeker has fled and applied for protection but is waiting for a decision (1); a refugee has been recognised as needing protection and cannot be sent back to danger (1).
3. Any two: jobs; higher wages; better healthcare or education; family already living there; safety and political freedom; a safer environment.
4. Money sent home by migrants to their families (1).
5. They are fleeing, so push factors dominate and they go to the nearest safe place (1); they often travel on foot with few resources and cannot afford long journeys (1); they hope to return home when it is safe (1); neighbouring countries may share language or culture (1). Max 3.
6. Economic: unemployment and low wages, so people cannot support their families (1). Social: poor healthcare and education (1). Political: war, persecution or discrimination threaten safety (1). Environmental: drought, floods or other hazards destroy farms and homes (1). Credit developed explanation.
7. Remittances raise family incomes and pay for food, schooling and housing (1), and are a large share of some economies, e.g. 45% of Tajikistan's GDP (1); less unemployment and less pressure on services (1); returning migrants bring savings, skills and ideas (1).
8. Dangerous journeys, often through people smugglers (1); low-paid, insecure work and exploitation (1); discrimination or racism (1); separation from family (1); qualifications not recognised and language barriers (1). Max 4.
9. Immigrants are mostly young adults of working age (1), so they fill labour shortages, e.g. in care work (1), and pay taxes that help fund pensions and healthcare for the elderly (1). Immigrants may also raise the birth rate (1). Max 3.
10. Applicants are scored on characteristics such as skills, qualifications, language and a job offer (1); points are added up (1); only those above a set threshold are given a visa (1).
11. Level-marked. Strengths: reduces irregular crossings at that point; shows the government is acting, which may reduce public tension. Weaknesses: very expensive to build and patrol; pushes migrants to longer, more dangerous routes and into the hands of smugglers, raising deaths; does nothing about push factors; refugees still have a legal right to claim asylum. Judgement expected: can reduce crossings locally but treats the symptom, not the cause, and is neither effective in the long term nor sustainable on its own.
12. Level-marked. Credit a range: points-based systems (select skilled migrants, but control who comes rather than how many — UK record net migration of almost a million a year in 2023); quotas and temporary visas (fill seasonal gaps, exploitation risk); free movement (efficient labour market, no control of numbers, drains poorer members); border controls (expensive, divert to dangerous routes); international agreements (EU–Turkey 2016 cut Greek sea arrivals from 857,000 in 2015 to 30,000 in 2017, but depended on Turkey and left people in overcrowded camps); resettlement and integration (faster contribution, but fewer than 1 in 100 refugees resettled a year); reducing push factors (tackles causes — most sustainable — but slow). Judgement expected: destination controls manage who enters but not why people move; the most effective and sustainable approach combines legal routes, integration and development in origin countries.

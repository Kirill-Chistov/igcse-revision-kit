2. They only produce electricity when the wind blows or the sun shines (1), so output varies and cannot always meet demand without storage or back-up (1).
3. Water, sand and chemicals are pumped at high pressure into shale rock (1), cracking it (1) to release gas or oil that could not otherwise be extracted (1).
4. Two developed from: reservoir floods land and displaces people; destroys habitats; very expensive; traps sediment downstream; output falls in drought — 1 + 1 each.
5. Two from: developing renewables (wind, solar farms); exploiting new reserves (deep-sea drilling, fracking); nuclear power; import deals/pipelines; subsidies for renewables — 1 + 1 each.
6. Cheap, simple technology local people can maintain (1); reduce fuelwood cutting and so deforestation (1); less indoor smoke / better health (1); biogas uses dung, a local waste product (1).
7. Level marking. *L1 (1–3):* describes strategies. *L2 (4–6):* explains what several strategies achieve and their costs/limits. *L3 (7–9):* evaluates, comparing supply-side and demand-side approaches, judges sustainability (renewables and efficiency vs fracking), and reaches a supported conclusion.
8. Sweden, 2022: hydropower 28.5% (largest), other renewables 24.6%, oil 21.9%, nuclear 20.2%, coal 3.1%, gas 1.3% (1); about three-quarters low-carbon / little fossil fuel because it has no significant reserves (1); nuclear + hydro give 80% of electricity (1).
9. Level marking. *L1 (1–3):* simple statements (carbon tax, renewables, efficiency). *L2 (4–6):* developed statements — what each strategy does and why it helps. *L3 (7):* developed and accurate, with Swedish detail: carbon tax since 1991 ("polluter pays"); green-electricity certificates; ~500 district heating schemes; efficiency (229.8 → 215.7 GJ per person, 2010–22; 50% more efficient by 2030); interconnectors with Norway, Poland, Germany and gas from Denmark; storage for seasonal demand; targets of 100% renewable electricity by 2040 and zero carbon by 2045.

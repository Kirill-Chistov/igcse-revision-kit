## 1. Strategies (7.3.1)

**Strategies** are the plans, policies and agreements that decide what will be done; **techniques**
are the practical methods used to do it (§2).

- **City master plans and zoning** — decide where housing, industry, transport and green space may go.
- **National policies that spread growth** — investment in rural areas, regional towns or new towns, so
  that one city does not take all the migrants.
- **Slum-upgrading programmes** — partnerships between city governments, residents' groups, NGOs and
  lenders such as the World Bank.
- **Green-belt and urban-growth-boundary policies** — a legal limit on where the city may expand.
- **International agreements** — UN Sustainable Development Goal 11 (cities that are inclusive, safe,
  resilient and sustainable, by 2030) and the UN New Urban Agenda (2016).

::: definition
**Sustainable urban management** — improving life for today's residents in ways that are affordable to
keep going, include the poorest, and do not damage the environment for future generations.
:::

## 2. Techniques and how well each one works (7.3.1)

### Tackling the causes

| Technique | How it works | Cost or weakness | Judgement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rural development** — irrigation, rural schools, clinics, roads, microcredit | Reduces the push factors, so fewer people need to migrate | Slow and costly; the city's pull remains, and natural increase continues anyway | The only technique that treats the **cause**. **Sustainable** long term, but never enough on its own |
| **New towns and growth poles** — planned towns or new industrial centres away from the main city | Diverts migrants and investment from the overcrowded core | Very expensive; they stay half-empty if jobs do not move there too | Can work when jobs are moved with the people; otherwise money wasted |

### Housing and unplanned settlements

| Technique | How it works | Cost or weakness | Judgement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clearance and relocation** — demolish the settlement and rehouse residents, often in public apartment blocks | Removes unsafe housing quickly; frees land | Destroys homes, businesses and community ties; new flats are often far from work, so people drift back to new informal sites | Treats the symptom and moves the problem. **Least sustainable** socially |
| **Site-and-service schemes** | The authority provides plots with water, sewer, electricity and a road; residents build their own homes, often with a cheap loan | Plots may be on the edge, far from jobs; the poorest cannot afford the plot or the loan | Cheap for the government, and residents gain legal tenure and services. **Fairly sustainable** |
| **Self-help schemes** | Residents are given materials, tools and training, and build or improve homes themselves | Slow; quality depends on each household's time and money | Low cost, builds skills and ownership. **Sustainable** where materials keep coming |
| **In-situ upgrading** | The settlement is improved where it stands: paved paths, drains, piped water, toilets, street lights, legal land titles | Does not remove overcrowding or hazardous sites; rising values can push the poorest out | Keeps communities and jobs in place at a fraction of rebuilding cost; secure tenure makes residents invest. **Most sustainable** housing option |

### Transport and waste

| Technique | How it works | Cost or weakness | Judgement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mass transit** — metro, light rail, **bus rapid transit (BRT)**: buses in dedicated lanes with raised stations and pay-before-boarding | Moves large numbers quickly; cuts congestion and emissions | High build cost (metro far more than BRT); fares may exclude the poorest; takes road space from cars | Best value where it is affordable; BRT gives much of a metro's capacity at a fraction of the cost. **Sustainable** |
| **Traffic restraint** — congestion charging, park and ride, cycle lanes | Makes driving into the centre less attractive; the charge funds public transport | Unpopular; only works if good public transport already exists | Effective with mass transit, not instead of it |
| **Waste collection and recycling** — formal collection, sanitary landfill, recycling and composting, waste pickers organised into cooperatives | Removes waste before it blocks drains or spreads disease; turns waste into income | Costly to run; landfills fill up and give off methane; recycling markets fluctuate | Collection protects health; recycling and cooperatives make it **sustainable** by adding income and cutting landfill |

### Controlling sprawl

| Technique | How it works | Cost or weakness | Judgement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Green belt / urban growth boundary** | A zone of protected land around the city where building is restricted | Raises land and house prices inside; building leaps beyond the belt, lengthening commutes | Protects farmland and habitats, and forces reuse of land inside the city |
| **Brownfield redevelopment and densification** | Build on previously used land, and at higher density, instead of on greenfield sites | Clearing and decontaminating old sites is expensive; cramming can lose urban green space | Saves countryside and makes public transport viable. **Sustainable** if green space is kept |

## 3. Reaching a judgement (7.3.1)

Judge each technique on four tests: does it tackle the **cause** or only the symptom; does it help the
**poorest** or move them on; can the city **afford to keep it going**; and what does it do to the
**environment**?

On those tests, **top-down techniques that remove people — clearance and relocation — score
worst**: costly, disruptive and short-lived. **Bottom-up, low-cost techniques that keep communities in
place — in-situ upgrading, site-and-service and self-help — are the most sustainable**, especially when
combined with mass transit and brownfield-first planning. But they manage the symptoms. Only rural
development and falling birth rates slow growth itself, and they take decades. In LICs the limit is
usually **money and governance**: cities grow faster than their budgets.

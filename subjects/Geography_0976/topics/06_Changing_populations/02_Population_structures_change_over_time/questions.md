# Population structures change over time — Questions

Specification points 6.2.1–6.2.3. Cambridge command words. Marks in brackets.
These are practice questions written for this repository, not official Cambridge papers;
the guidance below shows the kind of point that earns credit.

## 1. Recall and definition

1. Define **population structure**. (1)
2. State the formula for the **dependency ratio**. (2)
3. State **two** causes of an ageing population. (2)

## 2. Data and interpretation

4. A country has 12 million people aged 0–14, 30 million aged 15–64 and 3 million aged 65 and over. **Calculate** its dependency ratio. (2)
5. Using the pyramids for Niger and Japan (Figure 1 in the notes), **compare** their population structures. (4)
6. **Suggest** why Qatar's pyramid has a large bulge of males aged 25–44. (3)

## 3. Understanding and explanation

7. **Explain** how natural increase influences the shape of a population pyramid. (4)
8. **Explain** how emigration can change the population structure of the country of origin. (3)
9. **Explain** the problems a youthful population can cause for a low-income country. (4)
10. **Explain** the economic impacts of an ageing population. (4)

## 4. Evaluation and judgement

11. "An ageing population is a greater problem than a youthful population." **To what extent** do you agree? (8)

## 5. Detailed specific example

12. For a named country you have studied, explain the reasons for its population growth or decline and describe the impacts of its population policy. (8)

## Answers and marking guidance

1. The make-up of a population by age and sex (1).
2. (Population aged 0–14 + population aged 65 and over) (1) ÷ population aged 15–64 × 100 (1).
3. Any two: low birth rate; rising life expectancy; emigration of young adults; large baby-boom generations reaching 65.
4. (12 + 3) ÷ 30 × 100 (1) = 50 (1).
5. Niger has a much wider base — 47% under 15 against 11% in Japan (1); Niger's sides slope in steeply to a narrow top, Japan's top is wide (1) — 3% against 30% aged 65+ (1); Japan has more women than men in the oldest groups (1); Niger's dependency ratio is higher (97 against 70) (1). Max 4; at least one comparative statement with data needed for full marks.
6. Immigrant workers (1) mostly young men recruited for construction and oil industry jobs (1); they come without families, and the female bars are not affected in the same way (1).
7. The birth rate sets the width of the base — a high birth rate gives a wide base (1), a falling birth rate a narrowing base (1). The death rate and life expectancy set the sides and top — high death rates give steep sides and a narrow top (1), longer life straighter sides and a wider top (1). Baby booms show as bulges that move up the pyramid over time (1). Max 4.
8. Emigrants are mostly young adults (1), so the working-age bars show a gap (1); fewer people of child-bearing age means fewer births, so the base narrows later (1); the remaining population is older (1). Max 3.
9. High dependency ratio (1); heavy demand for school places and child healthcare the government cannot afford (1); too few jobs when the young reach working age (1), leading to unemployment, emigration or unrest (1); continued rapid growth as the young become parents, adding pressure on food, water and housing (1). Max 4.
10. Fewer workers for each pensioner (1); rising cost of pensions, healthcare and care (1), paid for by higher taxes on a shrinking workforce (1); labour shortages slow the economy (1); some positive: older people's spending creates jobs in care and leisure (1). Max 4.
11. Level-marked. For: ageing costs rise year after year and are hard to reverse; fewer workers must support more pensioners through higher taxes; labour shortages; most serious where a country ages before it is rich (China). Against: ageing countries are usually rich and can adapt — raise the pension age, attract immigrant workers, automate (Japan); a youthful population in a poor country cannot be given schools and jobs, and brings unemployment, poverty and continued growth; but a youthful population can become a demographic dividend if birth rates fall and jobs are created. Judgement expected: it depends on the country's wealth and preparation — the decisive factor is the size and productivity of the working-age population relative to its dependants.

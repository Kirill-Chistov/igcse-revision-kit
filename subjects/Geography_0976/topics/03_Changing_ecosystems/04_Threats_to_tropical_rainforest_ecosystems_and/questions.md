## Answers and marking guidance

1. The cutting down or burning of forest, usually for another land use, without replanting (1).
2. Any three: cattle ranching; commercial farming (soya, oil palm); subsistence farming / settlements;
   logging; mining / oil / gas; road and railway building; hydroelectric power; fire. (3)
3. Roads give access to remote forest (1); loggers, farmers and settlers follow them (1); clearance
   spreads out from the road in a fishbone pattern (1).
4. Beef is in high demand, so ranching is profitable (1); forest is cut and burned for pasture (1); the
   poor soil soon wears out, so more forest is cleared (1).
5. The reservoir floods large areas of forest (1); habitats are destroyed (1); drowned vegetation rots
   and releases methane (1); people living there are displaced (1). Max 3.
6. Most nutrients are stored in the trees, not the soil (1); when trees are removed or burned, the store
   is lost (1); without a canopy or roots, heavy rain leaches and erodes the soil (1); within a few years
   the soil is infertile (1).
7. No canopy to intercept rain (1), so more rain reaches the ground quickly as surface runoff (1),
   carrying soil that silts up rivers, so rivers flood downstream (1).
8. Burning and decay release CO₂ (1); the forest can no longer absorb CO₂ as a carbon sink (1); more
   greenhouse gas enhances the greenhouse effect and warms the planet (1).
9. They lose land, homes and food sources (1); their culture and way of life are destroyed (1);
   conflict with settlers / diseases brought in by outsiders (1).
10. Level-marked. Benefits: income, jobs, exports and taxes; roads and services. Problems: soil erosion,
    flooding, less rain, global warming, species loss, indigenous people displaced, long-term resources
    lost. 5–6: both sides, with a judgement — e.g. benefits are short-term and local/national, costs
    long-term and global; 3–4: both sides, limited judgement; 1–2: one side / list.
11. Strategy: a plan, law or agreement, e.g. a protected area or REDD+ (1). Technique: a practical
    method, e.g. selective logging or agroforestry (1).
12. Only mature trees of chosen species are felled (1); the rest of the forest stays standing and
    regenerates (1), so it keeps its soil, habitats and future income (1).
13. Crops are grown beneath and between trees (1); the soil stays covered and nutrients keep cycling (1);
    farmers earn a living without clearing the forest (1).
14. Strengths: legal protection for large areas (1); indigenous territories have lower deforestation
    (1). Weaknesses: "paper parks" with illegal logging and mining if not patrolled (1). Judgement:
    sustainable only if enforced (1).
15. Level-marked. Evaluate at least three approaches: e.g. protection and enforcement (Brazil cut
    deforestation ~80% in 2004–12, then it rose when enforcement weakened); REDD+ / certification
    (market value, but small); selective logging, agroforestry, sustainable extraction, ecotourism
    (sustainable — forest worth more standing); replanting (slow, repairs rather than prevents).
    6–7: balanced evaluation with a judgement on sustainability; 3–5: some evaluation; 1–2: description.

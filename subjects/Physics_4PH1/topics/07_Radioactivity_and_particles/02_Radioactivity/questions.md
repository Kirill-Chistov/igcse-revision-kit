# Radioactivity — Questions

Specification points 7.2–7.16 · Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Physics 4PH1.

> These questions and the marking guidance are **written for this repository by AI**. They are not
> official Pearson questions and the guidance is not an official mark scheme.

## 1. Atoms and isotopes (7.2, 7.3)

1. Describe the structure of an atom. (3)
2. A nucleus is written <sup>37</sup><sub>17</sub>Cl. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom. (3)
3. Explain what is meant by isotopes, using chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 as an example. (2)

## 2. Decay and radiations (7.4, 7.5)

4. State what is meant by saying radioactive decay is random. (1)
5. Describe the nature of an alpha particle, a beta particle and a gamma ray. (3)
6. Explain why alpha particles are the least penetrating of the three radiations. (2)

## 3. Practical (7.6)

7. Describe an experiment to find which type of radiation a source emits. Include how you would make the results reliable and one safety precaution. (6)
8. A student's corrected count rates are: no absorber 320 counts/min; paper 318; 3 mm aluminium 2; 2 cm lead 1. Identify the radiation and explain. (2)

## 4. Nuclear equations (7.7, 7.8)

9. Copy and complete: <sup>210</sup><sub>84</sub>Po → <sup>?</sup><sub>82</sub>Pb + <sup>4</sup><sub>2</sub>He. (1)
10. Strontium-90 (Z = 38) decays by beta emission to yttrium (Y). Write the nuclear equation. (3)
11. State the effect on the mass number and atomic number of (a) gamma emission, (b) neutron emission. (2)

## 5. Detection and background (7.9, 7.10)

12. Name two ways of detecting ionising radiation. (2)
13. Explain two sources of background radiation, one from Earth and one from space. (4)

## 6. Half-life (7.11–7.13)

14. Define half-life. (1)
15. A source has an activity of 3200 Bq and a half-life of 5 hours. Calculate its activity after 1 day and 1 hour. (3)
16. The count rate of a sample falls from 480 to 60 counts per second in 45 minutes. Calculate the half-life. (2)
17. A detector reads 820 counts/min near a source; background is 20 counts/min. The half-life is 10 min. Calculate the reading 30 min later. (3)
18. Explain how to find a half-life from a graph of activity against time. (2)

## 7. Uses, contamination, dangers (7.14–7.16)

19. Explain why a gamma emitter with a short half-life is used as a medical tracer. (3)
20. Explain why beta radiation is used to monitor the thickness of paper, and why the source must have a long half-life. (3)
21. Describe the difference between contamination and irradiation. (2)
22. Explain why an alpha source is more dangerous inside the body than outside it. (3)
23. Describe the problems of disposing of radioactive waste and how the risks can be reduced. (4)

## Answers and marking guidance

One mark per creditable point (1).

1. Small central nucleus containing protons and neutrons (1); electrons orbit the nucleus (1); protons positive, neutrons neutral, electrons negative / nucleus holds almost all the mass (1).
2. 17 protons (1); 37 − 17 = 20 neutrons (1); 17 electrons (1).
3. Atoms of the same element / same number of protons (17) (1); different numbers of neutrons (18 and 20) so different mass numbers (1).
4. You cannot predict which nucleus will decay or when it will decay (1).
5. Alpha: 2 protons and 2 neutrons / a helium nucleus (1); beta: a high-speed electron emitted from the nucleus (1); gamma: an electromagnetic wave (1).
6. Alpha is the most strongly ionising (1); so it loses its energy over a very short distance (1).
7. Measure background count rate without the source (1); place source a fixed distance from a GM tube and counter and record count rate with no absorber (1); place paper, then aluminium (a few mm), then lead between them, recording the count rate each time (1); subtract background from each reading (1); interpretation — stopped by paper → α; by aluminium → β; only reduced by lead → γ (1); repeat and average / count over a long time because decay is random; OR use tongs / keep distance / minimise time / store in lead box (1). Max 6.
8. Beta (1); paper has no effect but a few mm of aluminium reduces it to (about) background / zero (1).
9. 206 (1).
10. <sup>90</sup><sub>38</sub>Sr → <sup>90</sup><sub>39</sub>Y + <sup>0</sup><sub>−1</sub>e; mass numbers correct (1); atomic number of Y = 39 (1); beta particle written correctly (1).
11. (a) No change to either (1). (b) Mass number decreases by 1, atomic number unchanged (1).
12. Geiger–Müller tube (and counter) (1); photographic film (1).
13. Earth: radon gas released from rocks (such as granite) collects in buildings and is breathed in (1)(1) — or radioactive isotopes in rocks/soil/building materials/food. Space: cosmic rays, high-energy particles from the Sun and stars (1); reach the ground through the atmosphere, more at high altitude (1).
14. The time taken for half the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay / for the activity to halve (1).
15. 25 h = 5 half-lives (1); 3200 ÷ 2⁵ (1) = 100 Bq (1).
16. 480 → 240 → 120 → 60 = 3 half-lives (1); 45 ÷ 3 = 15 min (1).
17. Corrected: 800 counts/min (1); 3 half-lives → 100 (1); reading = 100 + 20 = 120 counts/min (1).
18. Choose a value of activity and find the time at which it has fallen to half that value (1); the time interval between them is the half-life (repeat for other starting values and average) (1).
19. Gamma passes out of the body so it can be detected outside (1); gamma is weakly ionising so causes less damage (1); short half-life so the activity falls quickly and the patient's dose is limited (1).
20. Beta is partly absorbed by paper, so the count rate depends on the thickness (1); alpha would be completely stopped and gamma would pass through almost unaffected (1); long half-life so the count rate does not fall during use / changes only because of thickness (1).
21. Irradiation: exposed to radiation from an outside source; the object does not become radioactive (1). Contamination: radioactive material gets onto or into the object, which then continues to be exposed until it is removed or decays (1).
22. Outside, alpha is stopped by the skin / cannot reach living cells (1); inside, it is next to living cells (1); it is strongly ionising so deposits all its energy in nearby cells, causing damage / mutations (1).
23. Waste remains radioactive for thousands of years (1); could leak into groundwater / reach people or food chain (1); sealed in glass/steel containers (1); stored deep underground in geologically stable rock, shielded and monitored / kept secure (1).

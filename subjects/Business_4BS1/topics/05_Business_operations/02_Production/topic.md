## 1. Job, batch and flow production (5.2.1)

![Three lanes: job production — a customer order is made by skilled workers into one unique product; batch production — a batch of identical items, a change-over while machines are reset, then a batch of a different item; flow production — identical items moving without stopping along a production line through several stations](assets/fig_job_batch_flow.svg)

*Figure 1. Job: one-off. Batch: identical within a batch, different between batches. Flow: identical and non-stop.*

::: definition
**Job production** — making a single, unique product to an individual customer's order, e.g. a wedding dress or a bridge.

**Batch production** — making a group (batch) of identical products together, then changing the equipment to make a batch of a different product, e.g. bread or clothing.

**Flow production** — continuous production of large quantities of identical, standardised products on a production line, e.g. cars or soft drinks.
:::

**Impact of each type of production**

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job | • Meets the customer's exact needs → high satisfaction, premium price<br>• Varied, skilled work → motivated workers | • Highest cost per unit (skilled labour, no bulk buying)<br>• Slow; very small output |
| Batch | • Some variety — switch products to match demand<br>• Lower cost per unit than job (bulk buying, specialist machinery) | • Down time while machines are reset between batches<br>• Stocks of materials and finished batches → storage costs |
| Flow | • Lowest cost per unit (economies of scale, automation) → competitive prices<br>• Very high, consistent output and quality | • Very high set-up cost; inflexible — hard to change the product<br>• Repetitive work → low motivation; one breakdown stops the whole line |

Job production is usually **labour-intensive** and flow production **capital-intensive** (→ 5.3).

## 2. Productivity (5.2.1)

**Definition — Productivity:** the output produced per worker (or per unit of input) in a given period of time — a measure of how efficiently resources are used.

::: note | Formula — learn it (not on the exam formula list)
**Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers** (always give the time period).

12 workers bake 4 800 loaves a week → 4 800 ÷ 12 = **400 loaves per worker per week**.
After training, the same 12 workers bake 5 400 → 450 per worker: a rise of 50 ÷ 400 × 100 = **12.5%**.
:::

Productivity is raised by training, better technology and equipment, motivation and lean production (§ 3).

![Chain: productivity rises, so labour cost per unit falls; the business can then either cut prices to become more competitive and sell more, or keep prices and earn a higher profit margin; separately, fewer workers are needed for the same output, which may mean redundancies and lower morale](assets/fig_productivity_impact.svg)

*Figure 2. The impact of a productivity improvement.*

## 3. Lean production (5.2.2)

**Definition — Lean production:** methods of production that use as few resources as possible — materials, labour, time, space — by cutting out waste.

![Left: just-in-time as four steps — customer order received, components ordered, components arrive just when needed, product made and sent — with almost no stock held. Right: the Kaizen cycle — spot a small problem, workers suggest an improvement, make the small change, check the result, repeat](assets/fig_jit_kaizen.svg)

*Figure 3. JIT removes the waste of holding stock; Kaizen removes waste through many small, continuous improvements.*

**Definition — Just-in-time (JIT):** a method of stock control in which materials and components arrive exactly when they are needed for production, so little or no stock is held.

:::: grid
::: advantages | JIT — advantages
- Little stock → lower storage costs and less cash tied up in stock.
- No stock going out of date, damaged or obsolete → less waste.
:::
::: disadvantages | JIT — disadvantages
- Depends on reliable suppliers — one late delivery stops production.
- No buffer stock → cannot meet a sudden rise in demand.
- Frequent small orders → loses bulk-buying discounts.
:::
::::

**Definition — Kaizen:** continuous improvement — all employees regularly suggest and make small improvements to the way work is done.

:::: grid
::: advantages | Kaizen — advantages
- Many small gains add up → higher productivity and quality, lower costs, with little spending.
- Workers are involved and their ideas used → higher motivation.
:::
::: disadvantages | Kaizen — disadvantages
- Takes time — team meetings and training.
- Small steps may not solve a major problem that needs a big change.
- Needs every worker's commitment; some resist constant change.
:::
::::

**Why using resources effectively matters:**

- More output from the same resources, less waste → lower costs per unit → lower prices or higher profit margins.
- Less waste of materials and energy → better environmental image with customers.

## 4. Technology in production (5.2.3)

**Definition — Robotics (automation):** using programmable machines to carry out production tasks that people used to do.

:::: grid
::: advantages | Robotics — advantages
- **Productivity:** robots work 24/7 without breaks → more output, lower cost per unit.
- **Quality:** precise and consistent → fewer faults and less waste.
- Can do dangerous or repetitive tasks → safer workplace.
:::
::: disadvantages | Robotics — disadvantages
- **Cost:** high cost to buy, install, maintain and programme.
- **Flexibility:** hard to switch to a new or customised product; a breakdown halts output.
- Job losses → redundancy costs, lower morale, poor public image.
:::
::::

**Balancing cost, productivity, quality and flexibility.** The gains in productivity and quality must outweigh the cost and the lost flexibility, so the right level of technology depends on the business's situation:

| | More technology suits… | Less technology suits… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output | large volumes of identical products — high cost spread over many units | small volumes of varied or customised products — flexibility matters more |
| Wages | high | low |
| Finance | enough available for the investment | limited |
| Customers value | precision and consistency | hand-made or personal products |

## Common traps

- Productivity is output **per worker**, not total output: hiring more workers can raise output while productivity falls.
- A batch can be large; batch means identical items made together, then a change-over.
- JIT is about **when stock arrives**; Kaizen is **small, continuous** change — not one big change.

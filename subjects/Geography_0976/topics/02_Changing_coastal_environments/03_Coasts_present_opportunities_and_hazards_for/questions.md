## Answers and marking guidance

*Marking points written for this repository — not an official Cambridge mark scheme.*

**1.1** Any three: ports/trade; fishing; tourism; industry needing cooling water or flat land;
energy (offshore oil/gas, wind, wave, tidal); flat fertile land; attractive environment.

**1.2** Working with natural processes to reduce erosion/flooding (1).

**1.3** (a) typhoon (b) hurricane (c) cyclone.

**1.4** Any two: water above about 18 °C; shallow water / sunlight; clear water (little
sediment); salty water.

**2.1** Rotation of the Earth (Coriolis effect) is too weak near the Equator (1) so the storm
cannot start to spin (1).

**2.2** Very low pressure lets the sea surface rise (1); strong onshore winds push/pile water
against the coast (1); water floods low-lying land, worse at high tide (1).

**2.3** Groynes trap sediment moved by longshore drift (1); less sediment reaches beaches
further along (down-drift) (1); those beaches become narrower, so waves erode the land behind (1).

**2.4** Any two developed: nursery for fish → food/income (2); protect coast from storm surges
and erosion by absorbing wave energy (2); trap sediment and filter pollutants (2); store carbon (2);
timber, fuelwood, food for local people (2).

**2.5** Sea 1–2 °C above normal for weeks (1); corals expel the algae living in them (1); coral
turns white and starves, dying if the heat lasts (1).

**3.1** Hotel: hold the line with hard engineering, e.g. sea wall/rock armour (1), because the
property value justifies the cost (1). Farmland: managed retreat or do nothing (1), because
protection would cost more than the land is worth / soft option more sustainable (1).

**3.2** Rock armour (1). Strength: cheaper than a sea wall / absorbs wave energy (1). Weakness:
moved by storms / blocks access / looks out of place (1).

**3.3** Weaker, poorly built housing (1); fewer or less effective warnings and communications (1);
fewer shelters and less transport to evacuate (1); less money for emergency response, and people
live in exposed low-lying areas (1).

**4.1, 4.2, 4.5, 4.7 — levels guidance (7 marks).**
Level 1 (1–2): lists techniques/strategies with little explanation.
Level 2 (3–5): explains how several work with some strengths and weaknesses.
Level 3 (6–7): balanced strengths and weaknesses, uses sustainability (cost, cause vs symptom,
long-term, effect elsewhere) and reaches a supported judgement. In 4.5 and 4.7 detail from the
named example (Eastern Seaboard / Great Barrier Reef) is needed for Level 3.

**4.3** Between about 30°N and 30°S (1); in warm, shallow tropical seas (1); concentrated in the
Coral Triangle / SE Asia, Great Barrier Reef, Caribbean, Red Sea (1); few on west coasts with cold
currents or near large river mouths (1).

**4.4 (Eastern Seaboard)** Sea-level rise faster than the global average, +47 cm at New York City
since 1925 (1–2); subsidence ~10 mm per year raises relative sea level (1); low, flat relief (1);
impacts: beach retreat (Massachusetts), wetland loss (Louisiana, ~40 ha/day), flood risk, economic
damage made worse by hurricanes, e.g. Andrew 1992: 65 deaths, >$27 bn (1–3). Max 6.

**4.6 (Great Barrier Reef)** Importance: 9,000 species incl. 400 coral, 1,500 fish (1); habitats
(1); >2 million tourists/yr, >$8 bn (1); >60,000 jobs (1). Threats: bleaching from climate change,
declining water quality, coastal development, crown-of-thorns outbreaks destroying >90% of a
reef's coral (1–3). Max 6.

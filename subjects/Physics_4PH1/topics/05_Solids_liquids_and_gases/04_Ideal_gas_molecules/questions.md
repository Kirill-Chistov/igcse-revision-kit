# Ideal gas molecules — Questions

Specification points 5.15–5.22 · Topic 5. Marks in brackets.

## 1. Recall

1. State the value of absolute zero in °C and in K. (2)
2. Convert (a) 35 °C to K, (b) 150 K to °C. (2)
3. Complete: the Kelvin temperature of a gas is ______ to the average ______ energy of its
   molecules. (2)

## 2. Understanding and explanation

4. **[3 marks]** *Explain* how the molecules of a gas exert a pressure on the walls of its container.
5. Explain why increasing the temperature of a gas increases the average speed of its molecules. (2)
6. Explain why there is a lowest possible temperature. (3)
7. **[3 marks]** A gas is squashed into a smaller volume at constant temperature. *Explain* why its
   pressure increases.
8. **[3 marks]** A sealed can of gas is heated. *Explain* why the pressure inside increases.

## 3. Application

9. A gas at 120 kPa occupies 250 cm³. It is compressed at constant temperature to 100 cm³.
   Calculate the new pressure. (3)
10. The pressure of a gas in a sealed rigid container is 100 kPa at 27 °C. Calculate the pressure at
    127 °C. (3)
11. A bubble of volume 2.0 cm³ is released at a depth where the pressure is 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Calculate
    its volume at the surface, where the pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa (temperature constant). (2)

## 4. Higher demand

12. A student says: "If I heat a gas from 20 °C to 40 °C in a fixed volume, its pressure doubles."
    Explain why she is wrong and calculate the factor by which the pressure actually increases. (3)
13. The pressure of a fixed mass of gas at constant volume is measured at several temperatures between
    0 °C and 100 °C. Describe how a graph of the results can be used to find absolute zero. (3)

## Answers and marking guidance

> Marking guidance written for this repository, not an official Pearson mark scheme.

1. −273 °C (1); 0 K (1).
2. (a) 308 K (1); (b) −123 °C (1).
3. proportional (1); kinetic (1).
4. Molecules move randomly / in all directions (1); they collide with the walls and bounce off,
   exerting a force on the wall (1); pressure = total force ÷ area / many collisions per second over
   the area (1).
5. Higher temperature means greater average kinetic energy of the molecules (1); mass is unchanged,
   so their (average) speed is greater (KE = ½*mv*²) (1).
6. Cooling reduces the kinetic energy of the molecules / they slow down (1); kinetic energy cannot be
   less than zero (1); so there is a temperature (absolute zero, −273 °C) at which molecules stop
   moving / have no kinetic energy and it cannot get colder (1).
7. Temperature constant so molecules' average speed unchanged (1); same number of molecules in a
   smaller volume, so they hit the walls more often / more collisions per second per unit area (1);
   greater force per unit area, so greater pressure (1).
8. Molecules gain kinetic energy / move faster (1); they hit the walls more often (1) and with more
   force (1), so pressure increases (volume constant).
9. *p*₂ = *p*₁*V*₁ ÷ *V*₂ (1) = 120 × 250 ÷ 100 (1) = 300 kPa (1).
10. *T*₁ = 300 K, *T*₂ = 400 K (1); *p*₂ = 100 × 400 ÷ 300 (1) = 133 kPa (1).
11. *V*₂ = 3.0 × 10⁵ × 2.0 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁵ (1) = 6.0 cm³ (1).
12. Pressure is proportional to the Kelvin temperature, not the Celsius temperature (1);
    293 K → 313 K (1); factor = 313 ÷ 293 = 1.07 (1).
13. Plot pressure against temperature in °C and draw a straight line of best fit (1); extend
    (extrapolate) the line back to where pressure = 0 (1); the temperature there, about −273 °C, is
    absolute zero (1).

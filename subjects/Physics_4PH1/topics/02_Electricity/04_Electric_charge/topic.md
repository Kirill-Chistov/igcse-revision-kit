## 1. Conductors and insulators — Paper 2 only

| | Conductors | Insulators |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | **metals** (copper, aluminium, steel), graphite | **plastics** (polythene, PVC, acetate), rubber, glass, dry wood, dry air |
| Electrons | some are **free to move** through the material | held in place — **cannot move** through it |
| Charge put on it | flows away through it (and through a hand holding it) to earth | **stays where it was put** |

That is why only insulators can be charged by rubbing while you hold them.

## 2. Charging by friction — Paper 2 only

::: definition
An object becomes **negatively charged** when it **gains electrons** and **positively charged** when it
**loses electrons**. Only electrons move; the positive charges (protons) stay in the nuclei.
:::

Rubbing two different insulators together transfers electrons from one surface to the other by
friction. The two objects end up with **equal and opposite** charges.

![Left: a polythene rod rubbed with a cloth; electrons move from the cloth to the rod, so the rod gains electrons and becomes negative and the cloth loses electrons and becomes positive. Right: an acetate rod rubbed with a cloth; electrons move from the rod to the cloth, so the rod loses electrons and becomes positive and the cloth gains electrons and becomes negative.](assets/fig_charging_friction.svg)

*Figure 1. Charging by friction: which way the electrons move.*

## 3. Forces between charges — Paper 2 only

**Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.** The forces act without contact.

A charged object also attracts an **uncharged** one (section 5), so attraction does not prove that two
objects carry opposite charges — only **repulsion** proves that an object is charged, and with the
same sign.

## 4. Practical: charging insulators by friction — Paper 2 only

![A rubbed polythene rod hangs in a paper stirrup from a nylon thread so it can turn freely. Left: a second rubbed polythene rod brought near one end makes it turn away — like charges repel. Right: a rubbed acetate rod brought near makes it turn towards it — unlike charges attract.](assets/fig_rod_practical.svg)

*Figure 2. Testing charged rods.*

1. Rub a polythene rod with a dry cloth and hang it in a paper stirrup from a nylon thread, so it
   can turn freely.
2. Rub a second polythene rod and bring it near one end without touching: the hanging rod turns
   **away** — like charges repel.
3. Rub an acetate rod and bring it near: the hanging rod turns **towards** it — unlike charges attract.
4. Repeat with rods and cloths of other insulating materials. A rod that **repels** the hanging
   polythene rod is negative; one that repels a hanging acetate rod is positive.

**Variables:** independent — the materials rubbed together; dependent — whether (and how strongly)
the hanging rod is repelled or attracted; control — the number of rubs and the distance.
Everything must be **dry**: a damp surface conducts and the charge leaks away.

## 5. Electrostatic effects explained by electrons — Paper 2 only

![Left: a negatively charged balloon near an uncharged wall repels electrons in the wall away from its surface, leaving the nearby surface positive, so the balloon is attracted. Right: a negatively charged metal sphere on an insulating stand is connected to earth by a wire; electrons flow along the wire to earth until the sphere is neutral. A positive object is discharged the same way, with electrons flowing up from earth.](assets/fig_induction_earthing.svg)

*Figure 3. Attracting an uncharged object; discharging a charged one.*

- **Attracting uncharged objects.** A charged rod picks up small bits of paper in the same way: a
  **negative** rod repels electrons away from the near side of the paper, a **positive** rod attracts
  electrons to it. Either way the near side gets the opposite charge to the rod and is attracted.
- **Sparks and shocks.** When enough charge builds up, electrons jump across an air gap to (or from)
  a nearby earthed conductor as a **spark**. You can charge up walking on a nylon carpet; touching a
  metal door handle, electrons flow suddenly through you and you feel a small shock.

## 6. Danger: fuelling aircraft and tankers — Paper 2 only

![An aircraft being refuelled through a hose from a fuel tanker, with both connected to earth by earthing cables. The danger chain: fuel flows fast through the hose; friction transfers electrons; charge builds up, giving a large voltage; a spark jumps to earth; the spark ignites fuel vapour — fire or explosion.](assets/fig_refuelling.svg)

*Figure 4. Why refuelling is dangerous, and the earthing that prevents it.*

**Prevention.** Before fuelling starts, the aircraft (or road/ship tanker) and the fuel truck are
connected to each other and to **earth** by a conducting cable. Electrons flow to or from earth as
fast as charge is separated, so no charge builds up and no spark can jump.

## 7. Uses: photocopiers and inkjet printers — Paper 2 only

**Photocopier.** The drum is coated with a material that conducts only where light falls on it.

![Five stages of a photocopier: 1 the drum is given a positive charge all over; 2 light from the white parts of the page hits the drum and the charge leaks away there, so charge remains only where the page is dark; 3 negatively charged toner powder is attracted to the charged areas; 4 positively charged paper pulls the toner off the drum; 5 heat melts the toner onto the paper.](assets/fig_photocopier.svg)

*Figure 5. How a photocopier uses charge.*

**Inkjet printer.** Each drop is given a charge, then passes between two deflecting plates. It is
attracted to the oppositely charged plate and repelled by the other. A computer varies the **charge
given to each drop**, which sets how far it is deflected, so every drop lands in the right place.

![An inkjet printer: a nozzle produces a stream of ink drops; a charging electrode gives each drop a charge; the drops pass between two deflecting plates with a voltage across them, one positive and one negative; each charged drop is deflected towards the oppositely charged plate; drops with more charge are deflected more and hit the paper higher up.](assets/fig_inkjet.svg)

*Figure 6. How an inkjet printer places each drop.*

:::

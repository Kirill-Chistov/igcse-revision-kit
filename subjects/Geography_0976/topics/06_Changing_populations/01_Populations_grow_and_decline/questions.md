# Populations grow and decline — Questions

Specification points 6.1.1–6.1.4. Cambridge command words. Marks in brackets.
These are practice questions written for this repository, not official Cambridge papers;
the guidance below shows the kind of point that earns credit.

## 1. Recall and definition

1. Define **birth rate** and **fertility rate**. (2)
2. State what is meant by **natural increase**. (1)
3. State the **replacement level** of fertility. (1)
4. Define **anti-natalist policy**. (1)

## 2. Data and calculation

5. A country has a birth rate of 28 per 1,000 and a death rate of 8 per 1,000. **Calculate** its rate of natural increase as a percentage. (2)
6. Using Figure 1 in the notes, **describe** the trend in world population since 1750. (4)
7. Using Figure 2 in the notes, **describe** the global pattern of natural increase. (4)

## 3. Understanding and explanation

8. **Explain** why birth rates are high in many low-income countries. (4)
9. **Explain** why a country's population can grow even when deaths exceed births. (3)
10. **Explain** why the death rate falls in Stage 2 of the demographic transition model. (3)
11. **Explain** why the birth rate falls in Stage 3 of the demographic transition model. (4)

## 4. Evaluation and judgement

12. **Evaluate** the demographic transition model as a way of understanding population change. (6)
13. "Pro-natalist policies do not work." With reference to examples, **to what extent** do you agree? (8)

## Answers and marking guidance

1. Birth rate: the number of live births per 1,000 people per year (1). Fertility rate: the average number of children a woman has in her lifetime (1).
2. The birth rate minus the death rate (1).
3. 2.1 children per woman (1).
4. A government policy that aims to reduce the birth rate (1).
5. 28 − 8 = 20 per 1,000 (1) = 2.0% per year (1).
6. Very slow growth from 1750 to about 1900 (1); growth accelerates rapidly after 1950 (1); use of data, e.g. 1 billion in 1804, 8 billion in 2022 (1); each extra billion added faster — 123 years for the second, 11–14 years each for the fourth to eighth (1); the growth rate has fallen since the 1960s, so the curve is beginning to level off (1). Max 4.
7. Highest in sub-Saharan Africa, many countries over 2–3% a year (1), e.g. Niger 3.3% (1); moderate (0–2%) in South Asia, Latin America and the Middle East (1); natural decrease in Europe, Russia, Japan, South Korea and China (1); generally highest in the poorest countries and lowest in the richest (1). Max 4.
8. High infant mortality, so parents have more children to ensure some survive (1); children work on family farms (1) and support parents in old age where there are no pensions (1); girls leave school early and marry young, so have more years of child-bearing (1); limited access to or knowledge of contraception (1); religious or cultural value placed on large families (1). Max 4.
9. Population change = natural change + net migration (1). If immigration exceeds emigration (1) by more than the natural decrease, the population grows (1), e.g. Germany, which has had more deaths than births since 1972 but has grown through immigration.
10. Improved food supply (1); clean water and sanitation reduce disease (1); vaccination, medicines and hospitals (1). Credit development of any point.
11. Infant mortality has fallen, so fewer births are needed (1); contraception becomes available (1); women are educated and in paid work, so marry and have children later (1); urbanisation — children cost money rather than working on farms (1). Max 4.
12. Level-marked. Strengths: simple, allows countries to be compared; links population change to development; helps predict and plan (schools, care homes); fits the history of Europe and North America. Limitations: based on the European experience — LICs' death rates fell faster after 1950, so growth was more explosive; ignores migration; ignores culture, religion and government policy, e.g. China's one-child policy; ignores war, famine and disease; no time scale; says nothing beyond Stage 5. Judgement expected: a useful general framework for comparison and prediction, but a poor guide to the timing or exact path of any single country.
13. Level-marked. Agree: South Korea spent over US$200 billion from 2006, yet fertility fell from 1.13 to 0.72 (2023); payments change the timing of births more than the total; the causes of low fertility — housing and education costs, careers, long working hours — are bigger than any payment. Disagree: France's family policy kept fertility near 2.0 for years, among the highest in Europe; childcare that lets women combine work and children has some effect; South Korea's rate has risen slightly, to 0.80 in 2025. Judgement expected: pro-natalist policies can slow the fall in the birth rate when they tackle the real costs of raising children, but none has raised fertility back to replacement level — so the statement is largely true.

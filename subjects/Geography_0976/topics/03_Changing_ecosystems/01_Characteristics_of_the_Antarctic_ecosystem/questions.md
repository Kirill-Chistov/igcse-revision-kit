## Answers and marking guidance

1. Centred on the South Pole (1); almost all south of the Antarctic Circle / 66.5° S (1); surrounded
   by the Southern Ocean (1); Antarctic Peninsula extends north towards South America (1). Max 3.
2. The line (about 50°–60° S) where cold Antarctic surface water meets warmer water from the north (1);
   the sudden change in temperature is a natural boundary for marine life (1).
3. High latitude: winter months of 24-hour darkness, so no solar energy (1). The sun is always low in
   the sky (1), so its energy is spread over a large area (1) and passes through more atmosphere (1);
   snow and ice reflect much of it (1). Max 4.
4. Very cold air is dense and sinks (1), creating high pressure (1). Sinking air prevents clouds forming
   (1), so there is very little precipitation — under 50 mm a year inland (1).
5. Highest in summer, about −27 °C in December–January (1); falls rapidly Feb–Apr (1); stays below
   −57 °C from April to September (1); annual range about 33 °C (1). Max 3; credit use of figures.
6. Under 250 mm precipitation a year — under 50 mm inland (1); cold air holds little water vapour (1);
   high pressure / sinking air stops clouds forming (1).
7. The proportion of sunlight a surface reflects (1). Snow and ice have a high albedo (1), so most
   sunlight is reflected rather than heating the surface (1).
8. Any three: ice sheet covering ~98% / up to 4.8 km thick; floating ice shelves; seasonal sea ice
   (~3 to ~18 million km²); very little ice-free land / thin soils; icebergs; Southern Ocean. (3)
9. Ice sheet: thick ice resting on land (1). Ice shelf: the ice sheet's floating extension over the sea
   (1). Sea ice: frozen sea water, about 1 m thick, growing each winter (1).
10. Very sparse, on the under 1% of land free of ice (1); mosses, lichens and algae (1); only two native
    flowering plants — Antarctic hair grass and pearlwort (1); low-growing / cushions (1). Max 3.
11. Cold slows growth and decay (1), so damage such as trampling or spills takes decades to recover (1).
12. Biotic: living parts, e.g. krill / penguins / moss (2). Abiotic: non-living parts, e.g.
    temperature / sea ice / sunlight (2).
13. Krill eat phytoplankton and ice algae (1) and are eaten by fish, penguins, seals and whales (1), so
    they link producers to almost every consumer (1); a fall in krill affects the whole web (1).
14. Algae grow on the underside of sea ice (1); young krill feed on them through the winter (1); so less
    sea ice means less food and fewer krill (1).
15. Two of: thick fat / dense feathers — insulation (2); huddling — shares warmth and shelter from wind
    (2); heat exchange in feet and flippers — little heat lost to the ice (2).
16. Grow on ice-free land, mostly on the milder Peninsula (1); low cushions / tufts, out of the wind (1);
    photosynthesise at temperatures near 0 °C (1); grow and seed in the short summer (1); mosses and
    lichens go dormant when frozen or dry (1). Max 4.
17. Two developed links, 3 marks each, e.g. summer daylight (abiotic) → phytoplankton bloom → krill →
    whales arrive to feed (biotic); sea ice (abiotic) → ice algae → krill survival; cold (abiotic) → slow
    decomposition → almost no soil → only mosses and lichens; penguin droppings (biotic) fertilise the
    ground (abiotic) → more moss. Each: factor (1), link (1), consequence (1).

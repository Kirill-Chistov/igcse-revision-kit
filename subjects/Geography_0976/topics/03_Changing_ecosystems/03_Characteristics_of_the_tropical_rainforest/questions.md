## Answers and marking guidance

1. In a belt either side of the equator (1), mostly within about 10° N and S / between the tropics (1);
   largest areas in the Amazon Basin, Congo Basin and South-East Asia (1); smaller areas such as
   Central America, West Africa, Madagascar, NE Australia (1). Max 3.
2. Further from the equator there is a dry season / less rainfall (1), so trees cannot stay evergreen
   and forest gives way to savanna (1).
3. High temperatures all year, about 27–28 °C (1); very small annual range, under 2 °C (1); heavy
   rainfall in every month, over 100 mm (1); total about 2,100 mm (1); wettest Nov–Jan (1). Max 4;
   credit figures from the graph.
4. The sun is overhead or nearly overhead all year (1), so its energy is concentrated on a small area
   (1) and passes through less atmosphere (1); days are about 12 hours all year (1). Max 3.
5. The sun heats the ground intensely in the morning (1); water evaporates and is transpired (1); warm,
   moist air rises by convection (1), cools and condenses at dew point into cumulonimbus clouds (1),
   giving heavy afternoon rain and thunderstorms (1). Max 4.
6. Intense heating makes air rise (1), lowering pressure at the surface where the trade winds converge
   (the ITCZ) (1).
7. Emergents, canopy, under-canopy (understorey), shrub layer, forest floor / ground layer (3 for all
   in order; 2 for four; 1 for three).
8. Dark — only about 2% of the light reaches it (1); humid (1); few plants, covered in leaf
   litter that decomposes quickly (1).
9. Most nutrients are stored in the vegetation, not the soil (1); dead material decomposes in weeks and
   nutrients are taken up again quickly by roots (1); heavy rainfall leaches nutrients out of the soil
   (1); so the soil holds very little in reserve (1).
10. High temperatures and moisture all year (1) let fungi, bacteria and termites decompose litter
    quickly (1); leaves fall all year round (1); shallow roots take nutrients up fast (1). Max 3.
11. Trees transpire large amounts of water vapour (1), making the air humid (1); this moist air rises
    and forms convectional rain (1), so much of the rain is recycled water from the forest (1). Max 3.
12. Two of: buttress roots support tall trees with shallow roots (2); drip tips shed water so fungi and
    algae do not grow (2); lianas climb to the light (2); epiphytes grow high on branches to reach light
    (2); thin smooth bark — no insulation needed, hard for climbers to cling (2).
13. e.g. Sloth: slow metabolism saves energy on low-energy leaves (1); algae in fur camouflage it (1);
    long curved claws let it hang in the canopy (1). Spider monkey: prehensile tail and long limbs (2)
    to move through the canopy without descending (1). Poison dart frog: bright colours (1) warn
    predators (1) that its skin is poisonous (1).
14. Level-marked. Links such as: high temperature and rainfall (abiotic) → fast decomposition by
    fungi and bacteria (biotic) → nutrients held in biomass, poor soil (abiotic); transpiration by trees
    (biotic) → humid air → convectional rain (abiotic); canopy (biotic) → shade on the floor (abiotic
    light) → few plants below, lianas and epiphytes; animals pollinate and disperse seeds. 5–6: at least
    two developed two-way links; 3–4: links described; 1–2: list of factors.

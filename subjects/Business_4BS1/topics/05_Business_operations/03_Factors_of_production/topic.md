## 1. The factors of production and how their mix changes (5.3.1)

**Definition — Factors of production:** the resources used to produce goods and services — land, labour, capital and enterprise.

| Factor | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | natural resources | sites, raw materials, water, oil |
| Labour | the physical and mental effort of people at work | factory workers, designers, managers |
| Capital | man-made resources used to produce goods and services | machinery, tools, vehicles, buildings, computers |
| Enterprise | the skill and risk-taking of the entrepreneur who organises the other three factors | the owner who starts and runs the business |

**The changing relationship.** Enterprise decides the mix of land, labour and capital. Factors can be **substituted** for one another — above all **capital for labour** (machines and robots doing work people did). Production shifts towards capital when:

- new technology makes machines cheaper and able to do more tasks;
- wages rise, so labour becomes more expensive than machinery;
- customers want large volumes at low prices and consistent quality.

The result: fewer but more skilled workers (to run and maintain the machines), and more finance needed for capital.

## 2. Labour-intensive and capital-intensive activities (5.3.1, 5.2.1)

**Definition — Labour-intensive production:** production that uses a high proportion of labour compared with capital; labour is the main cost.

**Definition — Capital-intensive production:** production that uses a high proportion of capital (machinery, equipment, technology) compared with labour; capital is the main cost.

| | Labour-intensive | Capital-intensive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main factor used | people | machinery and technology |
| Main costs | wages | buying and maintaining machinery |
| Typical output | varied, personal or customised; smaller volumes | large volumes of standardised products |
| Typical activities | hairdressing, restaurants, hand-made crafts, fruit picking | car assembly, oil refining, bottling soft drinks |

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Labour-intensive | • Flexible — workers adapt to new or individual orders<br>• Low set-up cost<br>• Personal, hand-made quality → premium price | • High labour cost per unit where wages are high<br>• Slower; output and quality less consistent<br>• Staff must be recruited, trained and motivated |
| Capital-intensive | • High productivity → low cost per unit at high output<br>• Consistent quality; machines can run 24/7 | • Very high initial cost → needs finance<br>• Less flexible; breakdowns stop output<br>• Job losses → lower morale |

## Common traps

- **Capital** here means machinery and equipment, not money (→ 3.1 for capital as finance).
- Managers are **labour**; **enterprise** is the owner's risk-taking and organising.
- Capital-intensive does not mean no workers — it means a high proportion of capital compared with labour.

# Market research

## 1. Purpose of market research

::: definition
**Market research** — gathering, analysing and interpreting information about customers,
competitors and the market.
:::

| Purpose | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Identify and understand customer needs | Find out what customers want — features, quality, the price they will pay, where they buy — so products meet those needs and sell. |
| Identify gaps in the market | Find a customer need that no existing product meets; a product filling it faces little competition. |
| Reduce risk | Decisions rest on evidence, not guesses, so there is less chance of launching a product that does not sell. Research reduces risk; it cannot remove it. |
| Inform business decisions | Gives data for decisions on product, price, place and promotion, and on e.g. location or expansion. |

## 2. Methods of market research

::: definition
**Primary (field) research** — collecting new, first-hand information for a specific purpose.

**Secondary (desk) research** — using information that already exists, collected by someone
else for another purpose.
:::

| | Primary research | Secondary research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advantages | • Specific to the business's own questions<br>• Up to date<br>• Not available to competitors → can give a competitive advantage | • Cheap or free and quick to obtain<br>• Often based on very large samples (e.g. government data) |
| Disadvantages | • Expensive and time-consuming<br>• Needs skill to avoid bias; small samples may be unreliable | • May be out of date<br>• Not specific to the business's needs<br>• Available to competitors too |

### Primary methods

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Survey | The same questions put to a sample of people — face to face, by phone or online. | • Reaches a large sample → numerical data to analyse<br>• An interviewer can explain questions | • Interviewers cost time and money<br>• People refuse or rush answers<br>• Interviewer can bias answers |
| Questionnaire | Written questions people answer themselves. **Closed** = fixed choices; **open** = own words. | • Cheap way to reach many people, especially online<br>• Closed questions are easy to analyse | • Low response rate<br>• Unclear or leading questions give biased results<br>• No one to explain a question |
| Focus group | A small group from the target market discusses a product in depth. | • Detailed opinions and reasons why<br>• Can react to ideas or prototypes before launch | • Small group may not represent the market<br>• Costly per person<br>• Dominant members can sway the group |
| Observation | Watching what customers do, e.g. counting shoppers passing a site. | • Shows what people actually do, not what they say<br>• Cheap | • Does not explain why they behave that way<br>• Time-consuming; small sample |
| Test marketing | Selling a new product in one small area before the full launch. | • Shows real buying behaviour<br>• Problems fixed before the full launch → lower risk | • Expensive<br>• Competitors see the product and can react<br>• Test area may not be typical |

### Secondary sources

| Source | What it provides | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Internet | Competitors' websites and prices, reviews, articles. | • Fast and mostly free<br>• Huge range of information | • Accuracy of many sites is unknown<br>• Much is out of date or irrelevant |
| Market reports | Specialist firms' reports on a market: size, growth, trends, shares. | • Expert, detailed and usually reliable | • Expensive to buy<br>• Any competitor can buy the same report<br>• May not cover a small local market |
| Government reports | Official statistics, e.g. census, incomes, spending. | • Free<br>• Very large samples, reliable | • General — not about a specific product<br>• Can be several years old |

## 3. Using market research data

| | Qualitative data | Quantitative data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Opinions, feelings and reasons, in words — cannot easily be measured | Numerical data that can be measured, counted and analysed statistically |
| Collected by | Focus groups, open questions, interviews | Closed questions, sales figures, government statistics |
| Example | "The drink is too sweet for me." | 62% of those asked would pay €2. |
| Advantages | • Explains **why** customers think and act as they do → insight for designing products | • Easy to compare, show in graphs and spot trends<br>• Can come from large samples |
| Disadvantages | • Hard to analyse and compare<br>• Usually small samples; open to interpretation | • Shows **what**, not why<br>• Only as good as the questions asked |

### Social media

Businesses collect market research data on social media by:

- running polls and surveys on their pages;
- reading comments, reviews, likes and shares on their own and competitors' posts;
- using platform analytics — followers' age, location and interests, and how many react to each post.

:::: grid
::: advantages
- Cheap and fast → large amounts of up-to-date data
- Reaches the target market directly, e.g. young customers
- Unprompted opinions show new trends early
:::
::: disadvantages
- Users are not typical of all customers → biased sample
- Fake or extreme reviews → unreliable
- Huge volume of data takes time and skill to analyse
:::
::::

### Reliability of data

::: definition
**Reliable data** — data that is accurate and represents the whole market, so repeating the
research would give similar results.
:::

Data is less reliable when:

- the **sample** is too small or not representative of the target market;
- questions are **biased** or leading, or an interviewer influences answers;
- people give **dishonest** answers (e.g. say they would buy, then do not);
- secondary data is **out of date** or was collected for another purpose;
- online data includes fake reviews or only certain groups respond.

**Why it matters:** decisions on what to make, what to charge and how many to produce are based
on the data. Unreliable data → wrong decisions → wasted money, unsold inventory or missed sales,
so the research fails its purpose of reducing risk. Reliability improves with larger,
representative samples, clear unbiased questions and up-to-date sources.

## Common traps

- A questionnaire done online is still **primary**; primary or secondary depends on **who
  collected the data and why**, not on the medium.
- Qualitative and quantitative data can each come from primary **or** secondary research.

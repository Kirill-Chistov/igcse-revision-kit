# Biological molecules — Questions

**Specification points 2.7–2.14B** · questions marked (P2) are Paper 2 only

Marking guidance below is written for this repository; it is not an official mark scheme.

## Recall

1. Which element is present in proteins but not in carbohydrates or lipids?
2. Name the basic units that make up (a) glycogen, (b) a protein, (c) a lipid.
3. Give the reagent and the positive result for the test for (a) starch, (b) glucose,
   (c) protein.
4. Define *catalyst*.

## Understanding

5. Explain why each enzyme catalyses only one reaction.
6. Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction increases from 10 °C to 30 °C.
7. Explain why the rate falls steeply above the optimum temperature.
8. A student leaves an enzyme at 5 °C and then warms it to 35 °C. Predict what happens to its
   activity and explain why.

## Practical and data

9. In the amylase practical, why are the starch and amylase solutions left in the water bath for
   5 minutes before mixing?
10. At 30 °C the iodine stayed orange-brown after 150 s. Calculate the rate in s⁻¹.
11. Describe a control for the amylase practical and state what it shows.
12. (P2) Describe how you would adapt the practical to investigate the effect of pH. Include how
    you would keep temperature constant.
13. Suggest two improvements to the method that would give more precise times.

## Exam-style

14. **[3 marks]** *Describe* how to test a sample of milk for fat.
15. **[4 marks]** *Explain* how a change in pH can reduce the activity of an enzyme.

## Answers and marking guidance

1. Nitrogen.
2. (a) simple sugars / glucose; (b) amino acids; (c) fatty acids and glycerol.
3. (a) iodine: orange-brown → blue-black; (b) Benedict's + heat: blue → brick-red (green,
   yellow, orange); (c) biuret: blue → purple.
4. A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up/changed.
5. The active site has a specific shape (1); only a substrate with a complementary shape fits (1).
6. Molecules have more kinetic energy / move faster (1); more collisions between enzyme and
   substrate (1); more enzyme–substrate complexes formed per second (1).
7. Bonds holding the enzyme's shape break (1); the active site changes shape (1); the substrate
   no longer fits / enzyme denatured (1).
8. Activity increases (1); the enzyme was not denatured at 5 °C, only slowed / low kinetic energy
   (1).
9. So both reach the chosen temperature before the reaction starts (1).
10. 1/150 = 0.0067 s⁻¹ (accept 6.7 × 10⁻³).
11. Use boiled (denatured) amylase (1); starch remains / iodine stays blue-black, showing the
    active enzyme digests the starch (1).
12. Add a buffer of a different pH to each starch–amylase mixture (1); same volume of buffer each
    time (1); keep all tubes in a water bath at one temperature, e.g. 35 °C (1); record time for
    iodine to stay orange-brown at each pH (1).
13. Any two: take samples more often (e.g. every 10 s); repeat and calculate a mean; use a colour
    reference / colorimeter for the end-point; start the clock at the exact moment of mixing.
14. Shake the milk with ethanol (1); pour the liquid into water (1); cloudy white emulsion shows
    fat (1).
15. Each enzyme has an optimum pH (1); a change in pH affects the bonds holding the enzyme's shape
    (1); the active site changes shape (1); substrate no longer fits / fewer enzyme–substrate
    complexes, enzyme denatured at extremes (1).
